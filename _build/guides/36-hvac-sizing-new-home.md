## How HVAC should be sized

The industry standard is a load calculation called ACCA Manual J, followed by Manual S to pick the equipment. A Department of Energy fact sheet on right-sizing warns against sizing by floor area or by a contractor's gut feel.

A load calculation looks at the actual house: insulation, windows, which way they face, air leakage, ductwork, and local design temperatures. Two homes with the same square footage can need very different systems.

## Why bigger isn't better

It's tempting to think a bigger system means more comfort. In a humid climate, it often means less.

The DOE fact sheet explains that an oversized unit starts and stops too often, which it compares to a car in stop-and-go traffic. That short cycling:

- **Hurts humidity control.** The system cools the air before it has time to pull moisture out, so the house feels cool and clammy.
- **Wastes energy.** One study it cites found units oversized by 50% or more used about 9% more cooling electricity a year.
- **Wears parts faster.** Frequent starts strain components.

## Is a 3 ton AC too big for 1500 square feet?

It might be, but square footage alone can't tell you. A well-insulated, tight 1,500 square foot home may need less. A leaky one with lots of west-facing glass may need more.

If you're buying a new home, ask whether a Manual J load calculation was done. If you're replacing a system in an older home, ask the contractor to do one instead of matching the old unit's size.

## Is 4 ton AC enough for a 3000 sq ft house?

Same answer. It can be, especially in a newer, efficient home, but only a load calculation will tell you. Newer homes built to current codes usually need less capacity per square foot than older homes did.

## What is the $5000 rule for HVAC systems?

It's an informal rule of thumb some contractors use for repair-or-replace decisions: multiply the age of the system by the repair cost, and if the result tops $5,000, consider replacing instead. It's a gut check, not a standard. Efficiency, refrigerant type, comfort problems and how long you'll stay matter too.

On a new home, it won't come up for years. InterNACHI puts a heat pump's typical life at 10 to 15 years with regular maintenance.

## Heat pumps and the ratings

Most new homes in our region use heat pumps. Census data shows 63% of new homes in the South had one in 2025. The DOE explains that a heat pump moves heat instead of making it, and can deliver up to three times more heat energy than the electricity it uses.

Two ratings matter:

- **SEER (now SEER2):** cooling efficiency. In warm climates, the DOE says it matters more.
- **HSPF (now HSPF2):** heating efficiency. In colder climates, it matters more.

North Carolina has real summers and real winters, so weigh both.

## Keep it working

- Change filters on schedule.
- Keep the outdoor unit clear of plants and debris.
- Have it serviced. The DOE notes that refrigerant charge and airflow affect efficiency, and systems should be checked for leaks at service calls.
