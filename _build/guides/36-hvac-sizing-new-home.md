## How HVAC should be sized

The industry standard is a load calculation called ACCA Manual J. Then Manual S is used to pick the equipment. A Department of Energy fact sheet warns against sizing a system by square footage or a contractor's gut feeling.

A load calculation looks at the real house: insulation, windows, which way they face, air leaks, ducts, and the local weather. Two homes the same size can need very different systems.

## Why bigger isn't better

It's tempting to think a bigger system means more comfort. In a humid place like North Carolina, it often means less.

The DOE fact sheet explains that a system that's too big starts and stops too often, like a car in stop-and-go traffic. That causes three problems:

- **Sticky air.** The system cools the air before it has time to pull the moisture out, so the house feels cool and clammy.
- **Wasted energy.** One study it cites found systems 50% or more too big used about 9% more cooling power a year.
- **Faster wear.** All those starts are hard on the parts.

## Is a 3 ton AC too big for 1500 square feet?

It might be, but square footage alone can't tell you. A tight, well-insulated 1,500 square foot home may need less. A leaky one with lots of west-facing windows may need more.

If you're buying a new home, ask if a Manual J load calculation was done. If you're replacing a system in an older home, ask the contractor to do one instead of just matching the old unit's size.

## Is 4 ton AC enough for a 3000 sq ft house?

Same answer. It can be, especially in a newer, efficient home, but only a load calculation will tell you. Homes built to today's codes usually need less system per square foot than older homes did.

## What is the $5000 rule for HVAC systems?

It's a rule of thumb some contractors use to decide between fixing and replacing. Multiply the system's age by the repair cost. If the answer is over $5,000, think about replacing it. It's a gut check, not a standard. Efficiency, comfort and how long you'll stay matter too.

On a new home, it won't come up for years. InterNACHI puts a heat pump's typical life at 10 to 15 years with regular upkeep.

## Heat pumps and the ratings

Most new homes in our area use heat pumps. Census data shows 63% of new homes in the South had one in 2025. The DOE explains that a heat pump moves heat instead of making it, and can deliver up to three times more heat than the electricity it uses.

Two ratings matter:

- **SEER2:** how efficient it is at cooling. The DOE says this matters more in warm places.
- **HSPF2:** how efficient it is at heating. This matters more in cold places.

North Carolina has real summers and real winters, so look at both.

## Keep it working

- Change the filter on schedule.
- Keep plants and leaves away from the outside unit.
- Have it serviced. The DOE notes that refrigerant and airflow affect efficiency, and leaks should be checked at service visits.
