## The objections, and the honest answer to each

I represent a builder, so take this as one side of the table. I've tried to give you answers you can use with a client without overselling anything.

### "The lots are too small."

Sometimes that's true. In 2025, 41% of new homes built for sale sat on lots under 7,000 square feet, according to the Census Bureau.

**Honest answer:** Lot sizes vary within a community. Shape, slope and what's behind the home matter as much as acreage. If a big yard is a must-have, resale may be the better fit, and that's fine.

### "New homes aren't built like they used to be."

Some are built better than others, at every age and price.

**Honest answer:** New homes are built to current codes and inspected by local building officials at set stages. Your client can also hire an independent inspector, often once before drywall and once before closing, if the builder allows it. And a new home comes with a builder warranty.

### "There are no trees, and it's a construction zone."

Early in a community's life, that's real.

**Honest answer:** Ask what's planned around the homesite and when it's expected to be built out. A homesite backing up to open space or a finished section can feel very different from one in the middle of active construction.

### "We can't wait months for a house."

A home started from scratch takes time. The Census Bureau's data shows homes built for sale averaged about 7.4 months from permit to completion in 2025.

**Honest answer:** Many builders have homes already under construction or finished. Ask what's available and when it could close. For clients with a home to sell, the build time can work in their favor.

### "We don't want an HOA."

Most new communities have one.

**Honest answer:** Read the covenants and budget together. Some clients find the rules a deal-breaker. Others like what the dues cover. North Carolina's Planned Community Act requires owners to elect the board, with a majority of owners on it, no later than the end of the developer's control period.

## Why are new construction homes not selling?

When a client says this, ask where they heard it, then look at the local numbers: how many homes are available, how long they're taking to sell, and what incentives builders are offering.

When builders have more homes available, incentives tend to grow. That can be an opportunity for a buyer, not a warning sign.

## Is it better to buy an older home or new construction?

It depends on what your client values. Older homes often win on lot size and established trees. New homes win on condition, layout and efficiency, and on not facing big replacements soon. InterNACHI's lifespan chart puts a heat pump at 10 to 15 years and a tank water heater at 6 to 12, so a 15-year-old home can come with a near-term to-do list.

Line both up on total cost, not just price.

## Why shouldn't you buy a house right now?

Good reasons not to buy right now: the payment would be a stretch, savings would be gone after closing, a move is likely soon, or the job isn't stable. Those are real.

"Because rates might drop" or "because prices might fall" are predictions. Nobody makes those reliably.
