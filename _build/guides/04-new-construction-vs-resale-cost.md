## Is it better to buy a new construction or existing home?

It depends on what matters to you and how long you'll stay. Neither one wins on price alone.

A resale home often has a bigger yard, big trees and a street that's all settled in. It can sometimes close fast, too. A new home wins on condition, layout and energy use. At Collins Ridge, homes are built first, so many are ready now and can close soon as well.

The mistake I see most is comparing two prices and stopping there. A lower price on a 15-year-old house can hide a to-do list that runs into five figures. A higher price on a new house can leave out things you still have to buy. At Collins Ridge, that's the fridge, washer, dryer and blinds. Line both homes up the same way, and the answer gets clearer.

## The comparison that works

Put both homes side by side, and fill in the same rows for each.

| Row | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| Price | The list price, plus any homesite premium on the new home |
| Cash to close | Down payment and closing costs, minus any seller or builder help |
| Monthly payment | Loan, taxes, insurance, HOA and mortgage insurance |
| Utilities | The last 12 months of bills on the resale. Any energy rating on the new home |
| Big repairs | How old the roof, heat pump, water heater and appliances are on the resale |
| What's missing | Blinds, appliances, a fence, plants, a patio |
| Warranty | What each one covers, and for how long |

Get the monthly numbers from a lender, not from me. Your rate, taxes and insurance are your own.

## Why the age of each system matters so much

Every big part of a house wears out over time. InterNACHI, a home inspectors' group, lists how long things usually last with normal upkeep:

- Architectural shingle roof: about 30 years. Three-tab shingles: about 20.
- Heat pump: 10 to 15 years. Central air: 7 to 15 years.
- Tank water heater: 6 to 12 years.
- Dishwasher: about 9 years. Fridge: 9 to 13 years.
- Outside paint: 7 to 10 years.

Now picture a home built 12 to 15 years ago. The water heater is at the end of its life, or past it. The heat pump is close. The roof might have 5 to 15 years left. None of that shows in the price. All of it shows up in your first five years.

On a new home, those clocks start at zero. That's the real advantage, and it's worth putting a number on.

## Is it cheaper to build or buy a house in 2026?

Buying is usually cheaper and faster than a custom build on your own land. With a custom build, you pay for the land, the design, the permits and a builder yourself.

The Census Bureau tracks how long this takes. For homes finished in 2025, homes built for sale took about 7.4 months from permit to done. Custom homes took 11.7 months with a hired builder, and 14.3 months when the owner ran the job.

It also helps to know where a new home's price goes. A 2024 survey by the National Association of Home Builders found that building the house was about 64% of the average price. The finished lot was about 14%. The rest was overhead, loans, marketing, commissions and builder profit. A big builder spreads the cost of plans and land work across a whole community. A one-off custom home pays for all of it alone.

Buying from a production builder isn't a custom build. The homes are built first from tested floor plans. You pick one that's ready now, being built, or starting soon. It's priced more like buying than building.

## What lowers a home's value the most?

Condition does, and it's the part you control. Appraisers rate condition from C1 to C6. Fannie Mae calls C1 a new home no one has lived in, with no wear at all. Homes slide down the scale when upkeep gets put off.

What pulls value down most often:

- **Put-off upkeep.** Roof leaks, a failing heat pump, rot and water damage.
- **Systems near the end of their life.** Buyers take the cost to replace them off the price.
- **Work done without permits.** It can slow a sale or need fixing before closing.
- **An old-style layout.** A closed-off kitchen or too few bathrooms is hard to fix.
- **Things about the spot you can't change.** Heavy traffic, noise, or a yard that drains poorly.

New homes can lose value too, if no one takes care of them. But you start at the top of the scale, not the middle.
