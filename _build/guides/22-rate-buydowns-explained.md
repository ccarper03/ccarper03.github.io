## Temporary vs. permanent, in plain words

**A temporary buydown** lowers your payment for the first year or few years. Money set aside at closing covers the difference. Then the rate steps up to the rate on your loan.

The most common kind is a 2-1 buydown. The rate is lower in year one, a little higher in year two, and back to the full rate from year three on.

**A permanent buydown** lowers the rate for the whole loan. You, or a seller, pay discount points at closing. The CFPB explains that one point equals 1% of the loan amount. Paying points lowers your rate, but costs more up front.

| | Temporary (like a 2-1) | Permanent (discount points) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How long the lower rate lasts | A set time, often 1 to 3 years | The whole loan |
| What it costs | The payment difference for those years | Points, each 1% of the loan |
| The rate you qualify at | The full rate | The lower rate |
| Best for | You want breathing room early on | You'll keep the loan a long time |

## Is a 2-1 buydown a good idea?

It can be, when it fits your plans. It helps if you want a lower payment while you settle in, or you expect your income to go up.

The catch: you have to be able to afford the full payment from day one. Fannie Mae requires lenders to qualify you at the full rate, not the lower one. That protects you, because in year three your payment goes to the full amount.

It also helps to ask what else the same money could do. A credit toward the price, or a permanent buydown, might be better if you plan to stay a long time.

## Who can pay for a 2-1 buydown?

A seller or builder, the lender, or you.

Under Fannie Mae's rules, a seller or builder can pay for a temporary buydown, within Fannie Mae's limits. The lender can also pay for it. Other loan programs have their own rules, so ask your lender.

## Does a 2-1 buydown require extra funds at closing?

Not from you, if the seller or builder is paying for it. The money is set aside at closing in a special account. Each month, part of your payment comes from that account. Fannie Mae requires the account to be fully funded when the loan is delivered.

If you pay for the buydown yourself, the cost is part of your cash to close.

## How does a seller pay for a 2-1 buydown?

The seller, or builder, pays the cost at closing, and it goes into the buydown account. It shows up on your Closing Disclosure.

Fannie Mae counts a buydown paid by the seller as a seller contribution. On a home you'll live in, those contributions are capped at 3%, 6% or 9% of the price or appraised value, depending on how much you borrow. The buydown has to fit under that cap along with any other credits.

Fannie Mae's other limits on temporary buydowns:

- The rate can go down by no more than 3 percentage points.
- The buydown can't last longer than 3 years.
- Your rate can go up no more than 1 percentage point a year.
- It applies to homes you'll live in and second homes, not investment homes.

## Buydown or price cut?

Ask your lender to show both side by side, over the time you expect to keep the loan:

- Your monthly payment in years one, two and three
- Your cash to close
- The total cost over 5 and 10 years

The CFPB suggests comparing options over the shortest, longest and most likely time you'll keep the loan. If you might refinance or move in a few years, a buydown that pays off over 15 years won't help you much.
