Framing a House: What to Look For

By Charles CarperUpdated October 20263 min read

Framing is the skeleton of the house: walls, floors, roof trusses and sheathing. Inspectors check that it's built to code, with proper headers, connectors, bracing and fire blocking, and that pipes and wires haven't weakened it. It's about 17% of a typical home's construction cost.

Why framing is the stage I love

I've cut the lumber, framed the walls and stood on the trusses. Framing is the stage where a slab turns into a house. Rooms appear in a day or two. You can stand in your future kitchen and see out the window openings.

It's also the stage where a lot gets decided that you'll never see again.

What do framing inspectors look for?

In North Carolina, building framing is one of the routine inspections the local building department performs, after rough-ins and before insulation. The inspector checks the structure against the code. Common items include:

  • Headers over windows and doors, sized for the span
  • Connectors and fasteners, like metal straps and clips that tie the roof to the walls
  • Wall bracing and sheathing, which keep the house from racking in wind
  • Fire blocking that slows fire from traveling through walls and floors
  • Notches and holes cut for pipes, wires and ducts, which can weaken framing if they're too big or in the wrong place
  • Stairs, including rise, run and headroom

A private inspector looking at the same stage, before drywall, adds a workmanship eye. InterNACHI's pre-drywall guidance lists things like stud and joist condition, notching, firestop material and missing metal clips.

How much would it cost to frame a 2000 sq ft house?

It depends on the plan, the roof, the lumber market and local labor. A simple box frames faster and cheaper than a home with lots of corners, vaulted ceilings or a complex roof.

For proportion, NAHB's 2024 construction cost survey found framing was about 16.6% of construction costs on the average new home. That's a better guide than a per-square-foot number, which changes with the lumber market.

What to look at yourself

If you get a walkthrough at the framing stage, look for:

  • Your layout. Doors, windows and walls where the plan says they should be.
  • Your structural options. A covered patio, an added window or a reconfigured room, if you chose one.
  • Blocking. Extra wood in the wall where you'll mount a TV, grab bars or heavy shelves.
  • Water exposure. Some rain on framing is normal. Standing water on subfloors or soaked framing that never dries isn't.
  • Damaged lumber. Cracked studs or broken trusses should be replaced, not ignored.

Take photos of every wall before insulation and drywall. You'll use them for years.

What should I check on my new build walkthrough checklist?

At the framing stage, use the list above. At the end, use a full room-by-room punch list covering doors, windows, outlets, plumbing, finishes and the exterior. My final walkthrough guide has one you can print.

A word on nail pops and cracks later

Framing lumber dries and shrinks a little after the house is closed in. A Canadian housing agency guide on drywall explains that as studs dry, they can pull away from the drywall, causing nail pops, and that differential shrinkage can cause cracks, often worst in the first year. That's normal settling, and most builder warranties address it.
