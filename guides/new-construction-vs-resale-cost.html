New Construction vs. Resale: What It Really Costs

By Charles CarperUpdated October 20265 min read

Compare the total cost, not the price tag. A resale home can cost less to buy and more to own, once you add the roof, heat pump and water heater it will need soon. A new home usually costs more up front and less to keep running in the early years.

Is it better to buy a new construction or existing home?

It depends on what matters to you and how long you'll stay. Neither one wins on price alone.

A resale home often has a bigger yard, big trees and a street that's all settled in. It can sometimes close fast, too. A new home wins on condition, layout and energy use. At Collins Ridge, homes are built first, so some may be ready now and can close soon as well.

The mistake I see most is comparing two prices and stopping there. A lower price on a 15-year-old house can hide a to-do list that runs into five figures. A higher price on a new house can leave out things you still have to buy. Ask for the standard features list to see what's included. Line both homes up the same way, and the answer gets clearer.

Get the monthly numbers from a lender, not from me. Your rate, taxes and insurance are your own.

Why the age of each system matters so much

Every big part of a house wears out over time. InterNACHI, a home inspectors' group, lists how long things usually last with normal upkeep:

  • Architectural shingle roof: about 30 years. Three-tab shingles: about 20.
  • Heat pump: 10 to 15 years. Central air: 7 to 15 years.
  • Tank water heater: 6 to 12 years.
  • Dishwasher: about 9 years. Fridge: 9 to 13 years.
  • Outside paint: 7 to 10 years.

Now picture a home built 12 to 15 years ago. The water heater is at the end of its life, or past it. The heat pump is close. The roof might have 5 to 15 years left. None of that shows in the price. All of it shows up in your first five years.

On a new home, those clocks start at zero. That's the real advantage, and it's worth putting a number on.

Is it cheaper to build or buy a house?

Buying is usually cheaper and faster than a custom build on your own land. With a custom build, you pay for the land, the design, the permits and a builder yourself.

The Census Bureau tracks how long this takes every year, and the pattern holds: homes built for sale finish faster than custom homes, and homes where the owner runs the job take the longest.

It also helps to know where a new home's price goes. The National Association of Home Builders' cost survey shows that building the house is the biggest share of a new home's price, followed by the finished lot. The rest is overhead, loans, marketing, commissions and builder profit. A big builder spreads the cost of plans and land work across a whole community. A one-off custom home pays for all of it alone.

Buying from a production builder isn't a custom build. The homes are built first from tested floor plans. You pick one that's ready now, being built, or starting soon. It's priced more like buying than building.

What lowers a home's value the most?

Condition does, and it's the part you control. Appraisers rate a home's condition, and a brand-new home no one has lived in gets the top rating. Homes slide down the scale when upkeep gets put off.

What pulls value down most often:

  • Put-off upkeep. Roof leaks, a failing heat pump, rot and water damage.
  • Systems near the end of their life. Buyers take the cost to replace them off the price.
  • Work done without permits. It can slow a sale or need fixing before closing.
  • An old-style layout. A closed-off kitchen or too few bathrooms is hard to fix.
  • Things about the spot you can't change. Heavy traffic, noise, or a yard that drains poorly.

New homes can lose value too, if no one takes care of them. But you start at the top of the scale, not the middle.

How I'd use this

If you're torn between a resale home and a new one, ask the listing agent how old the roof, heat pump and water heater are. Ask for the last year of power bills. Ask the builder what comes with the home and what doesn't. Then fill in the table above with real numbers from your lender.

Once a new home is in the running, the next question is how builder incentives fit into the price. At Collins Ridge, ask me what's available on the home you like.
