Design Center Upgrades: What's Worth It

By Charles CarperUpdated October 20263 min read

Spend on what's hard or messy to change after you move in: structure, layout, electrical, plumbing and anything behind drywall. Go easier on what you can swap later: light fixtures, faucets, paint and hardware. That rule saves more money than any single upgrade.

The one rule

I went through the design center myself when I bought new construction in 2009. Here's the rule I'd hand anyone walking in:

Do now what's hard to do later. Do later what's easy to do later.

Do it now Can wait
Structural options: a covered patio, extra windows, a room reconfiguration Light fixtures
Electrical: outlets, ceiling fan supports, extra circuits, TV and data wiring Faucets and cabinet hardware
Plumbing: a gas line, a utility sink, a hose bib Paint colors
Insulation or other energy upgrades Mirrors and accessories
Flooring that runs under cabinets or across most of the house Window treatments
Cabinet layout and height Backsplash, sometimes

How much do design center upgrades cost?

It varies widely by builder, community, plan and what you choose. Some buyers add little. Others add a meaningful share of the price.

Two things to know before you go:

  • Set a budget first. Decide your total before the appointment. It's easy to spend more one option at a time.
  • Ask how upgrades are paid for. Some are rolled into your price and loan. Some need a deposit when you sign. Ask which, and whether deposits are refundable.

What upgrades to skip on a new build?

The ones you can do later for less, or that only you will value:

  • Lighting and plumbing fixtures. Easy to swap after closing, often for less.
  • Premium paint colors. Paint is a weekend project.
  • Very personal style choices. Bold tile or a one-of-a-kind finish can narrow your buyer pool later.
  • Top-tier everything. Pick one or two places to splurge, not every room.

What upgrades increase home value the most?

The best guide is what buyers already say they want, and what holds value in resale.

NAHB's 2024 survey of buyers ranked these highest: a laundry room, a patio, ENERGY STAR windows, exterior lighting, a front porch, ceiling fans, garage storage, main-level hardwood flooring, a walk-in pantry, ENERGY STAR appliances and a full bath on the main level.

Remodeling data tells a similar story. The 2025 Cost vs. Value report for the South Atlantic region found modest projects recouped far more of their cost at resale than big ones. A midrange minor kitchen remodel recouped more than its cost, while a midrange major kitchen remodel recouped about half and an upscale one about a third. Exterior items like a new garage door or steel entry door recouped well over their cost.

The lesson carries to the design center: practical, broadly appealing upgrades hold value better than the most expensive finish in every room.

How I'd spend the budget

  1. Lock in structural, electrical and plumbing items first.
  2. Then flooring and cabinets, since they're expensive to change.
  3. Then one or two places you'll enjoy every day, often the kitchen or primary bath.
  4. Leave the rest for after you've lived there a while.
