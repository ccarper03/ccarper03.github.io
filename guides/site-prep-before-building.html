What Happens Before a House Goes Up: Site Prep

By Charles CarperUpdated October 20263 min read

Site prep is everything that happens before the foundation. Marking buried lines, clearing, keeping soil in place, shaping the ground so water runs away from the house, and bringing in water, sewer and power. It's a smaller share of the cost than the house itself, and it decides how your homesite handles rain for years.

What is included in site prep for construction?

On the job sites I worked, every house started with grading. Here's the usual order:

  1. Mark the buried lines. Before anyone digs, the pipes and wires already in the ground get marked.
  2. Keep the soil in place. Silt fence, gravel entrances and other steps keep dirt from washing off the site. This is called erosion control.
  3. Clear the homesite. Trees, brush and junk come out where the house, driveway and yard will go.
  4. Rough grade. The ground gets shaped so it's level where the house sits and slopes away from it.
  5. Bring in utilities. Water, sewer or septic, power, and sometimes gas run to the homesite.
  6. Get ready for the foundation. Digging for the footings and the slab or crawl space.
  7. Final grade, later. After the house is built, the ground gets its finished shape, and the yard is seeded or sodded.

Grading is what matters most to you over time. Water should run away from the house on every side.

How much does site prep cost for a house?

It depends on the homesite. A flat, open one costs much less than one that's sloped, wooded, rocky or needs extra dirt brought in.

For scale, site work is a smaller slice of the building cost than framing or finishes. NAHB's construction cost survey tracks the current share. In a builder's community, much of the big site work, like roads and utility lines, gets done for the whole neighborhood before homes start.

What is the first thing you should do before beginning to excavate a site?

Call 811. In North Carolina, NC811 tells the utility companies, who mark their buried lines for free. Check NC811's site for how much notice they need before you dig.

That goes for a builder starting a house, and for you planting a tree or putting up a fence later. Hitting a buried line is dangerous and costly.

Erosion control is required

North Carolina requires an approved plan to keep soil in place on larger jobs, before work starts. The state also sets deadlines to cover or plant bare ground after grading.

A builder's community is usually covered by one big plan. That's why you see silt fences and gravel entrances while homes are being built.

What it means for you as a buyer

  • Look at drainage. Where does water go on this homesite, and on the ones around it?
  • Ask about the grade. Is the homesite higher or lower than the street and the homes next to it?
  • Know what's next door. Homesites that haven't been graded yet will change.
  • See it finished. At Collins Ridge, homes are built first, so on a home that's ready now, you can see the final grade before you buy.
  • Keep the grade. After you move in, don't pile mulch or dirt against the foundation, or block the low spots that carry water away.
