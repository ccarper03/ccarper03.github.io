What Do HOA Fees Cover, and Are the Amenities Worth It?

By Charles CarperUpdated October 20263 min read

HOA fees usually pay for common areas, amenities like a pool or trails, the association's insurance, management and savings for future repairs. They usually don't cover the inside of your home or your own homeowners insurance. Judge an HOA by its budget, savings and rules, not just the monthly number.

What is the point of paying HOA fees?

An HOA pays for the parts of a community everyone shares and nobody owns alone. Most new communities have one. Most new single-family homes today are built in communities with an association.

What the dues usually cover:

  • Common areas. Entrances, community landscaping and shared open space.
  • Amenities. A pool, clubhouse, trails, a dog park or a playground.
  • Stormwater ponds and shared streets the town doesn't take care of.
  • The association's insurance on shared property.
  • Management.
  • Savings, called reserves, for future repairs.

In townhome sections, dues often cover outside items too, like lawns or the building outside. That's why townhome and single-family dues can be different in the same community.

What do HOA fees not cover?

Usually:

  • The inside of your home and the things you own
  • Your own homeowners insurance
  • Your yard, in most single-family sections
  • Utilities in your home
  • Special assessments, which are one-time charges when the budget falls short

Read the declaration to see what's included for your type of home.

What is the average monthly homeowners association (HOA) fee in North Carolina?

There isn't one good statewide number. Fees vary a lot with what the association covers. A community with a pool, clubhouse and paid management costs more to run than one with an entrance sign and a pond.

The right question isn't the average. It's what you get for the fee. Ask for the budget, and compare the dues with what's included.

What are the disadvantages of HOA fees?

  • They can go up. Budgets rise with insurance, upkeep and amenities.
  • Special assessments. A big repair can mean a one-time charge.
  • Rules. Fences, sheds, paint colors, parking and rentals may have limits.
  • The developer runs the board at first. North Carolina's Planned Community Act says owners must elect a board, with a majority of owners on it, no later than the end of the developer's control period.

How to judge if the amenities are worth it

  1. Will you use them? A pool you never visit is a cost, not a perk.
  2. Are they finished? Ask when planned amenities will open.
  3. Is the association saving enough? Ask what it's saving for future repairs.
  4. What's changed? Ask how dues have changed, and why.
  5. Can you live with the rules? Read them before you sign.

A note on builder credits

Loan programs let a seller cover some of a buyer's costs, within limits. If a builder offers help with HOA dues, ask your lender how it's applied.
