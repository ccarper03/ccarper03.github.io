How to Pick the Right Lot in a New Community

By Charles CarperUpdated October 20264 min read

At Collins Ridge, you pick a home and its homesite together. Look past size. Check how the lot drains, which way it faces, what's behind it, any easements, and what's planned for the land next door. Lots in new communities usually run smaller than in older neighborhoods.

How it works at Collins Ridge

Each homesite already has its floor plan and outside look set. So you're picking a home and its homesite together, not a lot and then a plan. Walk the homes that are ready now or being built, and use the checks below on each one.

What to check on every homesite

Size gets the attention, but these matter as much:

  • Drainage. Where does water go when it rains? Look at the lot after a storm if you can. The ground should slope away from the house on every side.
  • Slope. A sloped lot can mean steps, a walkout, or more foundation work.
  • What's behind you. Open space, another home, a road, a stormwater pond or a future phase.
  • Easements. Utility or drainage easements limit what you can build there, like a fence, shed or pool.
  • Orientation. Which way the back yard faces changes sun, shade and heat.
  • Flood risk. Look up the address on FEMA's Flood Map Service Center, which lets you search by address.
  • What's planned nearby. Ask for the community plan. Empty land won't stay empty.

What is considered a good lot size?

A good lot is one that fits how you'll use it. A big yard is great if you'll garden or have a dog. It's work if you won't.

For context, lots in new communities tend to run smaller than in older neighborhoods. NAHB publishes the current median lot size from Census data, if you want the number.

Is a 10,000 sq ft lot big?

For new construction today, it's on the larger side. It's about 0.23 acre, and many new homes sit on less.

Compared with older neighborhoods, it may feel ordinary. Lots have gotten smaller over time as land costs rose.

What's the average lot size in a subdivision?

It depends on the community and the region. In new communities, it often clusters close to the national median, with a range from smaller lots for townhomes or alley-loaded homes to larger corner or cul-de-sac lots.

Ask the builder for the range in the community you're considering, and where the lots you like fall in it.

When is a lot premium worth paying?

Many communities charge more for certain homesites. It can be worth it when the premium buys something you'll use or that buyers will pay for later:

  • More privacy, like backing up to open space
  • A flatter, more usable yard
  • Better orientation for your patio or garden
  • A quieter location away from busy streets

It's less worth it for size you won't use, or a view that could change when the next phase is built. Ask why a lot carries a premium and whether anything could change that.

Questions to ask before you choose

  • How does this lot drain, and where do the neighbors' downspouts go?
  • Are there easements, and where?
  • What's planned for the land behind and beside it?
  • Is it in a flood zone?
  • Which home and plan are set for this homesite?
  • Is there a premium, and why?
