Risks of Buying New Construction, and How to Handle Them

By Charles CarperUpdated October 20263 min read

The real risks are delays, build quality you can't see yet, a neighborhood still under construction, a developer-run HOA at first, and less negotiating room on price. Each one has a fix: get dates and terms in writing, inspect at key stages, read the HOA documents, and compare incentives instead of chasing a lower base price.

What are the downsides of buying a new construction home?

Here are the ones I'd want a friend to know about, and what to do about each.

Delays

Build dates move. Weather, inspections, materials and labor can all push a closing back. The Census Bureau's data shows homes built for sale averaged about 7.4 months from permit to completion in 2025, but your home isn't an average.

What to do: Ask what the contract says about the completion date and what happens if it moves. Plan your lease, rate lock and moving dates with some slack.

Quality you can't see yet

When you buy before the home is finished, you're trusting work that hasn't happened. Every home is built by many trade crews, and mistakes happen.

What to do: Ask what inspections happen and when. Local building inspectors check the work at set stages. You can also hire your own inspector, often once before drywall and once before closing, if the builder allows it. Then read the warranty so you know how fixes get handled after you move in.

A neighborhood still under construction

Early buyers live with construction traffic, noise and dust for a while. Trees are small. Amenities may not be finished.

What to do: Ask what's planned for the land around your homesite and when the amenities are expected. Look at the community plan, not just the model.

A developer-run HOA at first

In a planned community, the developer usually controls the homeowners association board for a period. North Carolina's Planned Community Act lets the declaration set up that period, and requires owners to elect a board, with a majority of owners, no later than when it ends.

What to do: Read the declaration, bylaws and budget before you sign. Ask when control is expected to pass to owners.

Less room on base price

Builders tend to protect the base price because it affects every home that sells after yours.

What to do: Focus on what can move: closing cost help, rate buydowns, options and the timing of a home that's already started.

A smaller yard than an older home

Many new homes sit on smaller lots. In 2025, 41% of new homes built for sale had a lot under 7,000 square feet, according to the Census Bureau.

What to do: Compare the homesites in the community. Size, shape, slope and what's behind you all vary.

What to know about buying new construction?

Three things save people the most trouble:

  1. The builder's sales consultant represents the builder. That's me, if you buy where I work. You can bring your own agent.
  2. The contract is the builder's contract. Read it, ask questions, and have it reviewed.
  3. The model isn't the standard home. Ask what's included and get it in writing.

Why are new houses so cheaply built?

Some are built better than others, at every price point. "New" doesn't mean cheap, and "old" doesn't mean solid.

Modern homes are built to current codes with engineered materials, which can look different from older construction without being worse. Production builders also standardize plans and buy at scale to control cost. Where quality slips, it's usually in execution: a trade crew that rushed or a step that got skipped.

I spent almost two years on job sites, from grading to final finishes. What I learned is that the best protection isn't the builder's reputation alone. It's inspections at the right stages, a punch list you take seriously, and a warranty you understand.
