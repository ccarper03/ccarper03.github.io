Risks of Buying New Construction, and How to Handle Them

By Charles CarperUpdated October 20264 min read

The real risks are delays, build quality you can't see yet, a neighborhood still being built, a developer-run HOA at first, and less room on price. Each one has a fix. Get dates and terms in writing, inspect at the right times, read the HOA documents, and compare incentives instead of chasing a lower price.

What are the downsides of buying a new construction home?

These are the ones I'd want a friend to know about, and what to do about each.

Delays

Build dates move. Weather, inspections, materials and crews can all push a closing back. The Census Bureau's data shows homes built for sale took about 7.4 months from permit to completion in 2025, but your home isn't an average.

What to do: Ask what the contract says about the finish date, and what happens if it moves. Plan your lease, rate lock and move with some extra time. Or pick a home that's ready now, and delays are off the table.

Quality you can't see yet

When you buy before a home is finished, you're trusting work that hasn't happened yet. Every home is built by many crews, and mistakes happen.

What to do: D.R. Horton builds first, so you can often buy a home that's done and see the work yourself. Local building inspectors check the work at set stages. You can also hire your own licensed inspector. At Collins Ridge, that can be once before drywall and once before closing, on our build schedule. Then read the warranty so you know how fixes are handled after you move in.

A neighborhood still being built

Early buyers live with construction traffic, noise and dust for a while. Trees are small. Amenities may not be done.

What to do: Ask what's planned for the land around your homesite and when the amenities will open. Look at the community plan, not just the model.

A developer-run HOA at first

In a planned community, the developer usually runs the homeowners association board for a while. North Carolina's Planned Community Act lets the declaration set that period. Owners must elect a board, with a majority of owners on it, no later than when it ends.

What to do: Read the declaration, bylaws and budget before you sign. Ask when control is expected to pass to owners.

Less room on price

Builders tend to protect the base price, because it affects every home that sells after yours.

What to do: Focus on what can move: closing cost help, rate buydowns, and the timing on a home that's already started. At Collins Ridge, ask me what's available on the home you like.

A smaller yard than an older home

Many new homes sit on smaller lots. In 2025, 41% of new homes built for sale had a lot under 7,000 square feet, according to the Census Bureau.

What to do: Compare the homesites in the community. Size, shape, slope and what's behind you all vary.

What to know about buying new construction?

Three things save people the most trouble:

  1. The builder's sales consultant represents the builder. That's me, if you buy where I work. You can bring your own agent.
  2. The contract is the builder's contract. Read it, ask questions, and have it reviewed.
  3. The model isn't the standard home. At Collins Ridge, the furniture, decor, fridge, washer, dryer and blinds in the model aren't included. Ask what comes with the home and get it in writing.

Why are new houses so cheaply built?

Some homes are built better than others, at every price. "New" doesn't mean cheap, and "old" doesn't mean solid.

Today's homes are built to current codes with engineered materials. They can look different from older homes without being worse. Big builders like D.R. Horton also use the same tested floor plans over and over and buy at scale, which is how they control cost. Where quality slips, it's usually a crew that rushed or a step that got skipped.

I spent almost two years on job sites, from grading to final finishes. What I learned: the best protection isn't the builder's name alone. It's inspections at the right times, a punch list you take seriously, and a warranty you understand.
